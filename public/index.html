How This Works Under the Hood

At a busy city grid, you can't let every intersection go green simultaneously — adjacent lights would send cars into each other. The challenge is to find the maximum number of intersections that can all be green at the same time, with zero conflicts. Mathematically this is the Maximum Independent Set (MIS) problem, which is NP-hard on classical computers — it explodes in difficulty as the city grows.
Each intersection is mapped to a single rubidium atom held in place by an optical tweezer laser. When we fire a carefully shaped laser pulse (an adiabatic sweep), nearby atoms experience the Rydberg Blockade: if two atoms sit within ~4.0 µm of each other, exciting one physically prevents the other from also becoming excited. The system evolves to its lowest-energy ground state, and that state naturally encodes the MIS solution as a pattern of excited (green = 1) and ground-state (red = 0) atoms.
After 500 measurements, the quantum device returns a probability distribution of bitstrings like 1010. Each digit maps to one node: 1 = Green Light, 0 = Red Light. The most frequently occurring valid bitstring is the hardware's best answer to "which intersections can all go green safely?" — shown highlighted on the grid above.
